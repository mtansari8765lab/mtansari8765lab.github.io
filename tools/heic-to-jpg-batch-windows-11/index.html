      Batch convert HEIC to JPG on Windows 11 — no app, no upload

      Windows 11 still does not open HEIC files out of the box. The usual fix is a codec from the Microsoft Store, which is a paid add-on on some systems and still leaves some desktop apps unable to open the files. Converting the photos to JPG is the reliable route instead — and doing a whole batch at once in your browser means no installing anything and no uploading your photos to a conversion website.

      This page converts HEIC to JPG entirely in your browser. The decoder is a file served from this site that runs on your own computer; your photos are read, decoded and re-encoded locally, and each JPG is offered as a download. We verified that by tracing the tool's code and watching its network traffic during a real conversion — no photo data leaves your machine. There is nothing to install, no account, and no Windows app involved at all: it works in Edge, Chrome and Firefox on Windows 11.

      How to convert a batch on Windows 11

      1. Select your HEIC files in File Explorer, then drag them onto the upload box — or click the box and pick several at once.
      2. Set the JPG quality. 92 is the default and suits most uses; 80 to 85 gives smaller files for email and the web.
      3. Click Convert to JPG. The first conversion loads the decoder (about 1.3 MB), then each photo converts in seconds.
      4. Download each JPG from the results list. There is no single ZIP download — you download the files one by one.

      How many files at once?

      We tested this page with 10 HEIC files in a single batch — all 10 converted, in under a second once the decoder was loaded. The tool itself sets no file-count limit, so a larger batch will still run, but keep batches to a sensible size: converting dozens of large photos at once uses more memory and makes the results list long to work through. Ten to twenty files per batch is a practical rhythm for a big folder — convert one set, download the JPGs, clear the list, and repeat.

      What you get, and what does not carry over

      Each JPG keeps the original filename with a .jpg ending, at the quality you chose. The photo's metadata — date taken, location and camera settings — is not carried into the JPG, so keep the original HEIC files if that record matters. Live Photos and burst sequences convert to their main still image only, and very saturated colours can shift slightly because the JPG is written in standard sRGB while HEIC can store a wider colour range.

      Troubleshooting

      • A file fails but the rest convert: one damaged or unusual HEIC does not stop the batch. The error names the file, so you can retry it on its own or keep the original.
      • The decoder is still loading: wait a moment and try again. The first conversion downloads the 1.3 MB decoder; later conversions start at once.
      • A file is skipped: only .heic and .heif files are accepted. Anything else is left out with a note.
      • Nothing converts at all: very old browsers without WebAssembly support cannot run the decoder. Use a current Edge, Chrome or Firefox.

      Frequently asked questions

      Do I need to install anything on Windows 11 to convert HEIC files?

      No. This is a browser tool, not a Windows app — there is nothing to install and no codec to buy. Open the page in Edge, Chrome or Firefox on Windows 11, drop your HEIC files in, and download the JPGs. The decoder runs in the browser on your own computer.

      How many HEIC files can I convert at once?

      We tested a batch of 10 HEIC files: all 10 converted in under a second once the decoder was loaded. The tool sets no file-count limit, so larger batches will run, but 10 to 20 files per batch is a practical rhythm — bigger batches use more memory and make a long results list. Each JPG downloads separately; there is no ZIP file.

      Are my photos uploaded to a server?

      No. The decoder is served from this site and runs in your browser, and we verified this by tracing the tool's code and watching its network traffic during a real conversion: no photo data leaves your machine. The page does load Google Analytics and advertising scripts, as most free sites do, but those never receive your photos.

      Why do some files fail while the rest convert?

      Usually because one file is damaged, half-downloaded, or an unusual HEIC variant the decoder cannot read. A failure never stops the batch: the error names the file, the rest still convert, and you can retry the failed file on its own. Files that are not HEIC at all are skipped when you add them.
